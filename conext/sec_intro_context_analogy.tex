However, recent efforts to leverage LLMs for misconfiguration detection~\cite{bogdanov2024leveraging,chen2024automatic,wang2024identifying,liu2024large, wang2024netconfeval, lian2023configuration} have missed an important insight:

human engineers do not examine entire configurations in a rigid, linear fashion; instead, engineers identify relevant configuration lines and dynamically ``jump'' to related parts of the configuration. Similarly, LLMs perform better when provided with {\em concise, relevant context} that mirrors this human approach. In this paper, we propose a framework for {\em efficiently extracting and feeding configuration context into LLMs} to address the aforementioned issues and improve misconfiguration detection in routers and switches. Specifically, we introduce the \emph{Context-Aware Iterative Prompting} (\sysname{}) framework, which tackles two key challenges: 

\mypara{Challenge 1---Automatic and Accurate Context Mining:}
Router configurations are long and complex~\cite{benson2009complexitymetrics}, with numerous interdependencies and a mix of pre-defined and user-defined parameters. For example, an interface may be configured with a specific MTU (which is within a pre-defined range of acceptable values) and an inbound packet filter (which refers to a named set of rules that have been defined by engineers elsewhere in the configuration). Depending on the scope of a configuration change, a domain expert may need to consider lines surrounding the change (\eg, the outbound packet filter applied to the interface), similar lines elsewhere in the configuration (\eg, the inbound packet filter applied to other interfaces), and/or lines that further define a parameter (\eg, the rules associated with the named packet filter). Automatically extracting and selecting such relevant context is essential for accurate analysis of configurations by LLMs. 
\textbf{Solution:} We model the configuration as a hierarchical tree, where each  line of the configuration corresponds to a path from the root to a parameter value. \aaron{Reword preceeding sentence.} \aaron{What about configuration lines that start stanzas (e.g., interface GE1/1/1)? Furthermore, we distinguish pre-defined and user-defined values based on the absence or existence, respectively, of the values as intermediate nodes in other paths, and refine these classifications with a majority-voting scheme \aaron{now LLM-based?} to ensure consistency across contexts.

\mypara{Challenge 2---Managing Context Overload in Prompts:} 
When faced with an overwhelming volume of irrelevant information, even the best experts can lose focus. Likewise, even after extracting relevant information, the quantity of required context can exceed a single prompt's capacity. \aaron{We should clarify that the capacity issue isn't just about token limits.} Overloading an LLM with excessive detail reduces coherence and degrades performance. \textbf{Solution:} In our iterative prompting framework, the LLM explicitly requests further context when needed, incrementally refining the prompt and focusing on the most critical lines. This approach mitigates overload and enables more precise detection.

We evaluate \sysname{} through two distinct case studies. First, we analyze real-world device configuration snapshots, inject realistic updates (including both correct modifications and latent misconfigurations), and demonstrate that \sysname{} outperforms partition-based LLM approaches, model checkers, and consistency checkers by at least 30\% in error detection accuracy. Second, we exhaustively evaluate \sysname{} on configuration snapshots from a medium-sized campus network. \sysname{} identifies over 20 previously overlooked misconfigurations, highlighting its practical utility.
